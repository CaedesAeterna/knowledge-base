**Overview**  
Nihilism is the view that life, values, or meaning lack inherent or objective grounding. It appears in several forms: metaphysical nihilism (denying concrete objects or existence), moral nihilism (denying objective moral values), and existential nihilism (the belief that life has no intrinsic meaning). Responses range from therapeutic acceptance to active rejection or reconstruction of value.

**Development Period**  
As an explicit philosophical theme, nihilism emerged in the 19th century alongside critiques of religion, morality, and metaphysics; it became prominent in literature and philosophy throughout the late 19th and 20th centuries as modernity raised questions about authority, meaning, and value.

**Key Figures** 
- Friedrich Nietzsche — Central figure often associated with diagnosing modern nihilism (the "death of God") and exploring its consequences; he both described nihilism and proposed cultural and philosophical responses (e.g., revaluation of values).
- Arthur Schopenhauer — Pessimistic metaphysics and critique of optimistic teleologies influenced later nihilistic themes about suffering, meaninglessness, and the will.
- Søren Kierkegaard — Though a critic of nihilism, his existential analyses of despair and the individual's relation to meaning shaped later existential and nihilistic debates.
- Ivan Turgenev — Literary figure whose novel Fathers and Sons popularized the term "nihilist" in the context of 19th-century Russian social and political radicalism.
- Max Stirner — Radical individualist whose critique of fixed moral and social ideals contributed to anti-authoritarian and nihilistic strains.
- Emil Cioran — 20th-century essayist known for aphoristic writings that confront despair, futility, and philosophical pessimism associated with existential nihilism.
- Albert Camus — Although opposed to nihilism, his work engaged central nihilistic themes (absurdity) and explored possible responses such as revolt and creation of personal meaning.
