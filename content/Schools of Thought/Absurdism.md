**Overview**  
Absurdism refers to the conflict between the human tendency to seek inherent value and meaning in life and the "silent," indifferent universe that offers none. It asserts that humanity is doomed to search for meaning where none exists. Unlike nihilism (which says there is no meaning) or existentialism (which says we create our own meaning), absurdism suggests we should embrace the absurdity of existence and live in defiance of it.

**Development Period**  
Developed primarily in the mid-20th Century (approx. 1940s–1960s), closely linked with the post-WWII literary and philosophical landscape of France. It is often seen as a distinct branch or reaction to existentialism.

Key Figures

- Albert Camus — French philosopher and novelist; articulated the core absurdist thesis (e.g., The Myth of Sisyphus) and advocated revolt, freedom, and passion as responses to the absurd.
- Søren Kierkegaard — 19th‑century Danish thinker; analyzed the existential tension and paradox of subjective faith versus an indifferent world, framing early discussions of the absurd.
- Franz Kafka — Czech‑writing novelist; his fiction dramatizes bureaucratic, metaphysical, and existential absurdity through alienation, incomprehensible systems, and surreal predicaments.
- Samuel Beckett — Irish playwright and novelist; dramatized the absurdity of human existence in minimalist, bleak, and darkly comic works (e.g., Waiting for Godot).
