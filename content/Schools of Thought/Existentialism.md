**Overview**  
Existentialism is a philosophical inquiry that explores the problem of human existence and centers on the lived experience of the thinking, feeling, acting individual. It emphasizes that the individual is solely responsible for giving meaning to life and living it passionately and sincerely ("authentically"). It often deals with the disorientation and confusion in the face of an apparently meaningless or absurd world.

**Development Period**  
While its roots can be traced back to the 19th century (approx. 1840s, with Kierkegaard and Nietzsche), it became a prominent philosophical movement in the early-to-mid 20th Century, particularly flourishing in Europe during and after World War II (approx. 1940s–1950s).

Key Figures

- Søren Kierkegaard — 19th‑century Danish thinker; emphasized individual faith, subjective truth, and the "stages" of life that shape authentic existence.
- Friedrich Nietzsche — German philosopher who critiqued traditional morality and religion, introduced the idea of life-affirming self-creation (e.g., "will to power," Übermensch).
- Jean‑Paul Sartre — 20th‑century French philosopher; developed radical freedom and responsibility, arguing that "existence precedes essence" and that individuals create their own values.
- Simone de Beauvoir — French existentialist and feminist; analyzed freedom, oppression, and the construction of gender, arguing for ethical responsibility in interpersonal relationships.
- Martin Heidegger — German thinker whose phenomenological analysis of Dasein (being‑there) explored authenticity, thrownness, and temporality as conditions of human existence.
- Karl Jaspers — German existential psychiatrist and philosopher; emphasized existential communication, transcendence, and existential limits in philosophical therapy.
- Gabriel Marcel — French Christian existentialist; focused on concrete intersubjectivity, hope, and fidelity as responses to alienation and existential despair.
